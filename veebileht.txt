

Estonia’s past is frequently summarized in national discourse as a history of “700 years of slavery”, a phrase mostly used by 19th century national thinkers to describe centuries of foreign domination. The expression, although it comes across as very strong and impactful, is not meant to be taken literally but symbolically, capturing a long history in which political power remained largely in the hands of external forces. Yet, this formulation can be misleading, as it may leave the impression that Estonian society began in a state of subordination. Even long before the 13th century crusader conquest, the region consisted of organized communities, regional governance structures, fortified settlements, and trade networks integrated into the Baltic and Northern world. Estonia was therefore not just a blank or “underdeveloped” territory awaiting civilization process, but a functioning society whose initial trajectory was redirected by conquests, some more violent than others.

The complexity of Estonia’s history lies precisely in this tension – a story of both early social formation and prolonged external rule. Over the centuries, control of Estonian territory has been passed between multiple foreign powers: German crusader states, Danish monarchs, Swedish administrators, and most infamously, Russian imperial authorities, while local political agency remained limited. Even after independence in 1918, the newly gained autonomy was interrupted by Soviet annexation from 1940 to 1991. The result is a historical experience shaped less by a single colonial regime than by a succession of externally imposed authorities. This multi-layered past complicates simple categorization of Estonia as just a colonized state and makes it a particularly revealing case for examining how domination, collective memory, and identity intersect. 

Estonia did not participate in overseas slavery or colonial expansion. Instead, its historical experience is characterized by being subjected to systems of domination that share structural features with colonialism. The decisive turning point occurred during the Northern Crusades of the early 13th century, when German military-religious orders conquered Estonian territory and established a new political order ruled largely by foreign elites. Indigenous populations became subordinated within an ethnically stratified system in which land, authority, and cultural prestige were concentrated in the hands of German-speaking rulers. Historians have noted that 19th and early 20th century intellectuals often described Livonia (Liivimaa) as a German colony dating back to its medieval christianization, presenting the Baltic region as a prototype of European colonialism. As old Estonian society was very connected to nature and practiced animism as a way of living, christianization affected a core part of the collective identity deeply. These interpretations did not function merely as historical descriptions. Rather, they served contemporary political purposes by providing Germany with a longer imperial genealogy and suggesting that its colonial ambitions had deep historical roots. Thus, the Baltic region became part of a broader narrative that portrayed German expansion eastward as a civilizing mission.

At the same time, Estonian national thinkers interpreted the same centuries of foreign rule as “700 years of slavery”, illustrating how identical historical conditions can generate opposing interpretations depending on political perspective. This historiographical divergence demonstrates that colonialism is not only a system of domination but also a discursive framework shaped by ideological needs. The Baltic example reveals that history narratives themselves can function as instruments of power. 

A brief timeline of foreign rule over Estonian territory:
Pre-1200s – Independent counties and regional polities
Local governance, trade networks, and fortified settlements.
1208-1227 – Crusader conquest
German military orders conquer Estonian lands.
13th-16th centuries – Crusader states and ecclesiastical rule
Foreign elites dominate administration and landholding.
1219-1346 – Danish rule in northern Estonia.
1561-1629 – Polish-Lithuanian and Swedish competition.
1629-1710 – Swedish Empire.
1710-1917 – Russian Empire; Baltic German nobility retains local authority.
1918-1940 – Independent Estonian Republic.
1940-1991 – Soviet rule (with Nazi occupation 1941–44).
1991-present – Restored independence.

This sequence shows that Estonia’s past of domination was cumulative rather than singular. Political authority repeatedly shifted between external powers, yet local populations remained largely excluded from governance. Such continuity explains why historiography later condensed these centuries into a single symbolic narrative of prolonged subordination and occupation.

One of the most significant institutions shaping Estonian society under foreign rule was serfdom. By the early modern period, peasants were bound to landowners, restricted in movement, and subjected to extensive labor obligations. Although this system differed legally from chattel slavery, it nonetheless constituted a coercive labor regime embedded in ethnic and social hierarchy. Baltic German elites held disproportionate economic and political power, reinforcing a stratified societal order that persisted even after the formal abolition of serfdom in the early 19th century. Historians continue to debate the degree of peasant freedom during specific periods, particularly under Swedish rule in the 17th century. Some argue that reforms placed crown peasants under royal jurisdiction and granted legal protections, while others emphasize that attachment to land and labor obligations largely remained. These disagreements highlight that systems of domination evolve rather than simply end, and that the memory of subordination is shaped by both legal structures and realistic views on everyday social relations, perhaps from the perspective of the peasants. 

The 20th century introduced a new form of external domination for Estonia. After annexation by the Soviet Union in 1940, Estonia experienced deportations, demographic restructuring, and centralized economic planning. Some scholars argue that ethnic grievances in late Soviet Estonia closely resemble patterns identified in theories of internal colonialism, particularly the existence of a core-periphery relationship and cultural division of labor structuring ethnic hierarchy. In such systems, different groups occupy unequal and symbolic positions, fostering collective identity and political mobilization. From this perspective, Soviet industrialization and migration policies contributed to structural inequalities that stimulated nationalist mobilization in the late 1980s. However, it is up to interpretation, as other scholars caution that the Baltic states were initially occupied rather than colonized and that colonial characteristics developed gradually rather than existed from the outset. They also note that postcolonial theory historically focused on Western overseas empires, making its application to the Soviet context analytically complex. Having said that, the theoretical analysis might be complex, but certain facts around this matter are not – Estonian cities were bombed down, people were taken straight from their homes to Siberia as they were treated like animals, Russian language became the state language and anyone who was not pleased with this became a prisoner, was sent to Siberia or suspiciously went “missing”.

Conflicts between Russian and Baltic historical narratives further complicate interpretation, since each narrative tends to neglect the legitimacy of the other. These debates of colonialism vs occupation of Estonia illustrate that the concept of colonialism in Estonia is not a settled classification, but an ongoing analytical and political question. In contemporary Estonia, historical experience plays a central role in national identity. Public discourse frequently emphasizes survival, endurance, and the restoration of independence after foreign rule. Museums, textbooks, and commemorative practices highlight deportations, repression, and resistance movements, writing the narrative of collective perseverance. 

At the same time, historians stress that memory is not neutral. Competing interpretations of the 20th-century remain visible, particularly between Estonian-language vs Russian-language communities – one's narrative invalidates another’s, and memory politics is a site of tension still causing societal divide to this day.  This persistence of these debates demonstrates how historical interpretation is shaped by present-day political concerns as much as by past events. More broadly, Eastern Europe has historically been positioned within Western discourse as an “internal other”, illustrating how colonial hierarchies can exist within Europe itself. This perception contributes to ongoing struggles over how Estonia’s past should be understood – whether as a story of slavery, occupation, colonization, or national awakening. 

Since regaining independence in 1991, Estonia has undertaken deliberate efforts to preserve and promote public memory of foreign dominance, particularly Soviet rule. Museums dedicated to occupation and resistance, national commemorative days, and educational curricula emphasize deportations, repression, and the struggle for sovereignty. These initiatives function not only as acts of remembrance but also as mechanisms for constructing national identity and legitimizing the modern state.

However, the institutionalization of memory has not been without controversy. One of the most visible examples is the relocation of the Bronze Soldier monument in Tallinn in 2007. Originally erected during the Soviet period as a memorial to Red Army soldiers who fought against Nazi Germany, the statue became a symbol of Soviet “liberation” for many Russian-speaking residents. For many ethnic Estonians, however, it symbolized Soviet occupation and repression. Its relocation from central Tallinn to the military cemetery triggered protests, riots, and diplomatic tensions with Russia. The episode revealed how a single monument can embody radically different historical perspectives and how memory politics can quickly escalate into broader political conflict.

More recently, symbolic changes in the cultural sphere (the Ukraine war) have also reflected ongoing renegotiations of memory and identity. The renaming of the Russian Theater in Tallinn to Südalinna teater (City Centre Theater) signals an attempt to reposition cultural institutions within a  more neutral civic framework rather than explicitly ethnic or linguistic categories. Only recently, in December 2022, Estonia passed a law requiring all public schools and kindergartens to switch to Estonian-language instruction. These developments illustrate how preservation policies extend beyond monuments and museums to encompass language, naming practices, and symbolic representation.

Together, these examples demonstrate that Estonia’s approach to historical memory is not static but continually evolving. The state actively institutionalizes narratives of occupation and resistance, yet for the time being, public space remains a site of negotiation where competing interpretations coexist. Preservation, in this sense, is not merely about safeguarding the past; it is about defining the moral and political boundaries of the present that may play a large role in the future.

In contemporary Spain, public engagement with the history of slavery and colonial exploitation has intensified in recent years, reflecting broader Western efforts to confront historical injustices. The Infàmia exhibition situates Catalonia within this global movement of reflection, emphasizing that many Western societies are reassessing their past involvement in slavery, human trafficking, and colonial exploitation. The exhibition explicitly framed itself as part of a process of analysis and symbolic reparation intended to promote education, social awareness, and a more inclusive understanding of history. 

This approach represents a relatively recent shift. For much of modern Spanish history, colonial expansion and maritime trade were commemorated primarily as sources of national prestige rather than examined critically as systems of exploitation. The Infàmia exhibition, therefore, reflects an institutional effort to reinterpret national heritage by foregrounding the violence and human suffering underlying economic success. Museums, exhibitions, and academic initiatives now seem to function as platforms for public reflection, demonstrating how cultural institutions can reshape historical consciousness. 

The exhibition’s detailed documentation of individual ships and traffickers illustrates this transformation in memory politics. For example, records of vessels reveal how traffickers transported hundreds of abducted individuals from African coastal trading posts to Cuba under inhumane conditions, with mortality rates estimated between 10% - 25% per voyage. Such case studies personalize the abstract history of slavery and confront viewers with concrete evidence of violence. Similarly, displays describing plantation life in Cuban ingenios expose the brutal labor regime endured by enslaved workers, whose working days could extend up to 20 hours during harvest seasons and whose life expectancy after arrival averaged only about 15 years. Exhibitions like this one do not simply present facts; they actively reinterpret historical memory. Their goal is to encourage contemporary audiences to recognize how past systems of exploitation continue to shape present inequalities. In this sense, Spain’s engagement with slavery increasingly takes the form of critical historical reflection rather than celebratory heritage.

In Estonia, by contrast, public memory focuses not on a perpetrator past but on experiences of subordination. Museums, memorials, and educational programs emphasize foreign domination, deportations, and resistance movements. While Spain confronts a history of participation in global systems of exploitation, Estonia institutionalizes remembrance of its own experiences of slavery, occupation, and possible colonization. Both countries use public institutions to shape historical consciousness, yet the moral orientation of these narratives differs significantly, as one emphasizes responsibility and the other survival.

The most fundamental differences between Spain and Estonia lie in their historical positions within global power structures. Spain functioned as a colonial power integrated into the transatlantic system of slavery, while Estonia historically claims a peripheral position within successive empires. This distinction is a crucial part of how each country remembers its past.
Spain’s colonial economy relied directly on enslaved labor. Plantation complexes in Cuba formed the backbone of a lucrative sugar industry owned largely by Spaniards or their descendants. In 1860 alone, the island contained more than 1300 such estates, all operating with enslaved labor. These systems generated immense wealth for colonial elites, who often lived in opulence while enslaved workers endured extreme deprivation. Spain’s involvement in slavery, therefore, represents not merely participation but structural dependence on forced labor. 

Estonia’s historical experience differs fundamentally. Rather than exploiting colonized populations abroad, Estonian territory itself was governed by external powers. Systems of domination existed internally through selfdom and imperial rule rather than externally through colonial plantations. As a result, Estonian historical narratives emphasize endurance under foreign authority rather than responsibility for oppression. Public awareness also differs. In Estonia, knowledge of foreign domination is widespread and strongly integrated into national identity. In Spain, awareness of colonial slavery has historically been less prominent in public discourse and has only recently become a subject of sustained institutionalized attention. The need for exhibitions such as Infàmia demonstrates that historical recognition is still developing.

Institutional policies reflect these contrasting trajectories. Estonia has invested in the memorialization of occupation and repression as a central component of state identity. Spain, by contrast, is still expanding initiatives dedicated to examining slavery and colonialism. Of course, all of this is based on the general Spanish past, and this one exhibition – if we were discussing Catalonia’s past, the discourse and direction of this essay would be much different.

Both countries face challenges in addressing their pasts, though the nature of these challenges differs. In Spain, the primary difficulty lies in confronting a history that implicates national institutions and the economic elite in systems of exploitation. While exhibitions and academic debates represent structural legacies such as racial inequality or colonial economic hierarchies. Public memory initiatives risk remaining confined to cultural institutions rather than transforming broader social understanding. 

Another challenge is the persistence of historical narratives that emphasize imperial glory while downplaying colonial violence. Because national identity is often tied to historical pride, confronting uncomfortable aspects of the past can generate resistance. The gradual emergence of critical exhibitions suggests that Spain is still negotiating how to integrate this history into its national narrative. Estonia faces different limitations. Its strong emphasis on victimhood and occupation can sometimes oversimplify historical complexity or marginalize alternative perspectives. Competing interpretations of the Soviet period remain visible within society, especially among the Russian-speaking population. It can be argued that Estonian national identity in this day and age could be more focused on our cultural heritage, spirit, and traditions, rather than making the key point a victim mindset. By saying that, I am in no way claiming that our occupational past does not need to be recognized, just that the national identity cannot only be about the nation’s sufferings.

Both cases illustrate that memory politics is inherently contested. Addressing historical injustice is not simply a matter of presenting facts; it requires negotiating between competing interpretations, identities, and political interests. 

Comparing Spain and Estonia reveals that confronting historical injustice requires different strategies depending on whether a society’s past is defined primarily by domination or by subordination. Spain demonstrates the importance of critical self-examination. By supporting exhibitions such as Infàmia, cultural institutions encourage public recognition of colonial violence and its lasting consequences. Estonia could draw inspiration from this willingness to scrutinize national history critically, particularly when addressing contested aspects of its own past. Still, some elements remain the same in both countries, like removing statues of colonialists/oppressors as a way of de-glorifying power and wealth built on others’ blood. 

At the same time, Estonia illustrates the value of institutionalizing memory through education, commemorations, and public discourse. Spain could learn from this approach by embedding reflection on slavery more deeply within national curricula and civic culture from an early age, rather than limiting it to museums or temporary exhibitions.

Ultimately, both cases show that historical memory is not static but continuously reshaped in response to present concerns. Spain’s growing engagement with colonialism and Estonia’s long-standing remembrance of domination demonstrate that confronting the past is an ongoing process rather than a completed task. Each country’s experience underscores the broader lesson that acknowledging historical injustice is essential not only for understanding the past but also for shaping more equitable societies in the present. Ultimately, whether a nation remembers itself as perpetrator or victim, meaningful engagement with the past requires critical reflection, openness to multiple perspectives, and the willingness to question inherited narratives. Only then can historical memory function not as a source of division, but as a foundation for a more self-aware and equitable future.
